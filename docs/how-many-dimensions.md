# How many dimensions?

Short answer: not a fixed number, and not 7. The count is decided by the data
under the rules below, and a person's result is more than their axis scores.

## What the data says so far

- **GSS 2016–2024:** 7 correlated dimensions fit best among the models tested.
- **ANES 2024:** one general left–right factor plus 9 specific dimensions
  (10 scores) beat 9 correlated dimensions, a 2-axis compass and a single axis
  on held-out answers.
- Parallel analysis on ANES suggests up to 18 factors, but past about 10 the
  extra factors have 2–3 items, low reliability, or are wording artifacts
  (the agree-keyed items forming their own "factor"). Adding them made the
  scores noisier, not more accurate.

## Why "more axes" is not automatically "more accurate"

An axis score is only useful if it is stable and means something. A dimension
measured by two questions might put you at the 80th percentile today and the
45th next week. That looks precise and isn't. A result with 25 axes, half of
them that unstable, describes a person *less* accurately than 12 solid ones.

## The rule: a dimension is reported only if it passes all five checks

1. **Reliability ≥ .70** for a typical quiz-taker (using the items people
   actually answer, not the whole bank).
2. **Improves held-out prediction**: adding it must predict people's hidden
   answers better, by more than bootstrap noise.
3. **Test–retest ≥ .70** over 2–4 weeks.
4. **Not a method artifact**: it must survive when agree/disagree keying is
   balanced or removed.
5. **Same meaning across groups**: items behave the same for people of
   different parties, ages, races and education at the same position (DIF).

Candidates that fail are kept in the issue map (layer 3 below), not reported
as axes.

## Domains not measured yet

The national surveys used so far barely ask about these, so their absence from
the current models says nothing about whether they are real dimensions:

- AI and technology regulation
- Privacy and surveillance
- Free speech and civil-liberties tolerance (GSS has it; ANES does not)
- Criminal justice and policing beyond the few ANES items
- Drug policy
- Religion in public life
- Education and parental rights
- Trade and economic nationalism (weak in ANES with only 2 items)
- Federalism (states vs federal government)
- Environment as distinct from general economic regulation

The quiz's own item bank needs 4–6 good items for each of these before the
rule above can test them. A reasonable expectation is one general factor plus
roughly 10–14 specific dimensions, but that number is an outcome of the
checks, not a target.

## What a person's result actually contains

1. **General left–right position**, the most reliable number (≈ .95).
2. **Specific dimensions**: where the person sits on each one *relative to
   what their general position predicts*. A strong economic left-winger
   with conservative views on guns shows up here, which a 2-axis chart
   would hide.
3. **The issue map**: every answer they gave, including exceptions and how
   firm each view is, compared with national answers. Nothing is averaged
   away; the axes summarize the map and never replace it.

Plus probabilistic group membership (which factions they resemble, with
percentages) and a coherence score (how consistently their answers follow any
single structure).
